MIT.nano successfully turned MIT’s 
nanotechnology, materials, and engineering 
systems into a single sustainable research 
center for health, energy, computing, and other 
emerging fields. This green building project 
focused on addressing humanity’s greatest 
challenges, including sustainable energy, clean 
water, clean air, and even finding a cure for 
cancer! The project incorporated sustainable 
strategies to develop a design that is highly 
transparent, energy-efficient and inviting for the 
community and potential stakeholders. Overall, 
the sustainable design and regenerative 
land use make MIT.nano a model for future 
campus planning. Before we move ahead and 
discuss the various sustainable measures and 
standards used to review the green building 
project, let’s credit everyone involved.

TEN STANDARDS OF AIA 
COTE TOP TEN A WARDS 
PROGRAM
The AIA COTE (Committee on the Environment) 
Top Ten Awards are given each year to the ten 
most innovative green building projects that set the 
standards in design and sustainability. It is one of 
the industry’s most prestigious awards programs 
that reward sustainability and sustainable design 
excellence. The committee evaluates the green 
building projects using ten standards/measures 
which it has developed over time.
1.Integration (to ensure a thoughtful process that 
delivers both beauty and function)
2.Equitable communities (to ensure the solutions 
positively impact future occupants and the larger 
community)
3.Ecosystems (to ensure the solution mutually 
benefits human and nonhuman inhabitants)
4.Water (to ensure proper improvement in the 
quality and conservation of water)
5.Economy (to ensure the design adds value 
for the owners, occupants, community, and the 
planet)
6.Energy (to ensure the design eliminates 
dependence on fossil fuels without compromising 
the project’s function)
7.Well-being (to ensure the design supports 
health and well-being for all people)
8.Resources (to ensure the design counts on 
sustainable and informed material and resource 
selection)
9.Change (to ensure the solution addresses the 
future risk and vulnerabilities)
10.Discovery (to ensure the project has lessons 
to learn for future designers, users, and operators)

1.Integration
Located nearby the iconic Infinite Corridor and the Great Dome, MIT.nano is home to class 100 and 
1,000 cleanrooms, imaging suites, and world-class laboratories. The project team aimed to achieve 
best-in-class performance in designing for maximum safety and occupant well-being, providing high 
flexibility and resilience, and making the site highly energy efficient.
The integrated sustainability approach to achieve the goals mentioned above helped the team 
revitalize the heart of the campus while maximizing collaboration and engagement across disciplines. 
The team reused an existing brownfield building site and transformed the service yard into an 
inspiring landscape. The project successfully developed a high-performance cleanroom design and 
achieved unprecedented overall sustainability.

3.Ecosystems
The project team had a massive challenge to 
overcome. The paved industrial brownfield site 
included a hazardous materials storage building 
that contaminated the soil. Transforming 
the site to retain, treat, infiltrate 
stormwater and regenerate the 
natural ecosystem was vital 
to MIT.nano’s well-being. At 
the same time, the team had 
to meet MIT’s urban heat-
island reduction goals without 
tampering with the site’s 
historical significance.

Landscape Ecology Masterplan 
effectively addresses issues like 
stormwater management, resilience 
planning, heat-island reduction, and more.

within the master plan. The project team planted 
over 100 trees that added a projected 25,000 
sq. ft. of vegetation.

areas - ensuring the water is well treated before 
it makes its way to the Charles River. The team 
began with only 7.9 percent of the site area 
supporting vegetation. Post project completion, 
it successfully vegetated over 35 percent 
of the site! Intentional design 
strategies like dark skies, bird 
safety, and specific regional

4.Water
Since MIT’s campus is surrounded by the 
Charles River and is near the Atlantic coast, the 
project site is built on tidal land reclaimed from 
the Charles River estuary. The area is covered 
in impervious surfaces and retains some of 
the river’s drainage patterns. Therefore, the 
stormwater runoff flows through the drainage 
into the Charles River.
The project team has installed low-flow and 
low-flush fixtures to ensure that the building 
consumes at least 49 percent less potable 
water. Since almost 64 percent of the site area 
is covered in impervious surfaces, the site 
irrigation water depends entirely on the reverse 
osmosis reject reclaim system. Furthermore, 
MIT’s new cooling towers (Central Utility Plant) 
significantly reduce particulate emissions and 
boost water conservation.
5.Economy
MIT.nano was also driven by 
strong economic factors. Multi-
million-dollar investments made 
the project’s core, which facilitates 
expensive high-performance space 
to monitor and regulate vibration, 
EMI, acoustics, temperature, and 
humidity. Most importantly, the 
team managed to establish such 
a facility at the heart of campus - 
eliminating the need for more of 
such investments.
The team used an intensive 
process to establish a life cycle cost 
analysis that included 140 Energy 
Conservation Measures (ECM). MIT.
nano included sixty of the ECMs 
based on the highest ROI and 
largest carbon reduction impact. The 
project team also had to consider programmatic changes over a period of 50-100 years. Therefore, 
they built robust MEP systems that included 12 percent additional capacity beyond full-built need.
The selection of stone cladding and terrazzo floors helped reduce maintenance costs and keep 
the project profitable throughout its life cycle. The construction costs per square foot amounted to 
$1,271/sq. ft. Meanwhile, the team saved $5.2M in construction costs and successfully reduced 
embodied carbon.

6.Energy
Research facilities and nanoscale laboratories are known for high energy consumption. To get the 
project LEED-certified, the team had to come up with innovative energy-efficient ideas. Moreover, 
MIT.nano required a particle-free ‘cleanroom’ environment that ticked all the boxes related to air 
filtration, temperature stability, humidity control, and ventilation.
The goal was simple - design the most energy-efficient academic cleanroom in the country. The 
team benchmarked several high-performance academic cleanrooms in the country and identified tool 
load assumptions, makeup-air (MAHU) efficiency, and recirculating-air (RAHU) efficiency as the three 
primary drivers of energy use. 
The team used high-impact strategies like exhaust heat recovery, variable volume exhaust and 
makeup, 100 percent filter coverage of cleanroom ceiling, variable volume RAHU (based on demand), 
class 100 cleanroom air change rate below 165 ACH, and local reheat with low-temperature makeup 
air. As a result, MIT.nano achieved a 51 percent energy cost savings and a 50 percent reduction in 
greenhouse gas emissions compared to the LEED ASHRAE 90.1 - 2007 baseline.

8.Resources
The selection of resources for MIT.nano focuses entirely on high durability and longevity. Although 
most nanoscale buildings are made with a cast-in-place reinforced concrete waffle slab structure, MIT.
nano shifted to a steel frame primary structure with composite concrete metal floors and roof decks. 
This move helped the team reduce the materials tonnage and save $5.2M in construction costs.
MIT.nano has stone cladding for exterior and interior surfaces, fiber cement boards, and terrazzo 
floors. It also uses 100 percent FSC-certified lumber that lowers embodied carbon insulation. 
Additionally, the project used a construction waste management plan which resulted in the diversion 
of 90 percent of construction waste from landfills. This helped the project earn a LEED exceptional 
performance credit.

9.Change
MIT.nano set a 
benchmark when it 
came to designing for 
adaptability. Since it 
is a science research 
facility, it was of 
prime importance 
to plan considering 
the rapid changes in 
research, instrumentation, 
and other crucial factors. 
Therefore, the project team ensured 
the programmatic addition of a nano-
makerspace for future research. 
The team focused on a modular design on 
an 11-foot module to allow future variations. 
MIT.nano has an additional MEP load 
capacity of 12 percent to accommodate 
future loads. The building is prepared for H5 
(high hazard) occupancy, which allows it to 
set up nano-research labs on upper levels.
Furthermore, the team included floor 
mitigation measures like slurry wall construction, elevating ground-floor level by six inches, relocating 
electrical switchgear on upper levels, elevating the generator fuel oil pumps six feet above the 
basement slab and relocating the emergency generator in the penthouse. 
10.Discovery
The findings from this LEED Platinum project have been presented firm-wide. The case study has 
been shared with several participating institutions and professional conferences like I2SL, ESTECH, 
SCUP, and Tradeline. The project team is preparing to put the unique energy conservation measures 
implemented at MIT.nano for future projects at Purdue University and Northeastern University. 
Post-Occupancy Evaluation (POE) is being performed while the project continually disseminates 
information. 
FINAL REMARKS
MIT.nano proves to be a benchmark for achieving sustainability goals while simultaneously 
maintaining MIT’s state-of-the-art practices for research and cleanroom design. The project’s 
commitment to advancing environmentally responsible design and planning helped turn the most 
energy-intensive building on the campus into the most energy-efficient. Besides the 2021 AIA COTE 
Top Ten award, MIT.nano was recognized for its significant achievements in advancing climate action 
and was awarded the 2019 Lab of the Year Award by R&D magazine, AIA Education Facility Design 
Award of Merit, ACEC Grand Award for Engineering Excellence, I²SL Go Beyond Award by the 
International Institute for Sustainable Laboratories, and Boston Society for Architecture Honor Award.
